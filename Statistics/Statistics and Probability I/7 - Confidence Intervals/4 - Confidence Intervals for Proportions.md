Recall that the general form of a confidence interval for the mean is an estimate plus or minus the standard error of the estimate times some critical value. The standard error is either the population or sample standard error divided by the square root of $n$, and the critical value is $z_{\alpha/2}$ if $\sigma$ is known and $t_{n-1,\alpha/2}$ otherwise.

Now, given some sample of size $n$, how can we create a confidence interval for a true proportion $p$? We can think of it as observations coming from $n$ Bernoulli trials with probability $p$. Now, we just have to estimate $\hat p$. By multiplying the pmfs, we get $L(p)=p^y(1-p)^{n-y}$, where $y$ is the number of successes. Now, $\ln L(p)=y\ln p+(n-y)\ln(1-p)$. Setting the derivative equal to zero gives us $\frac y p=\frac{n-y}{1-p}\to y-yp=np-yp\to \hat p=\frac y n$. This estimator is unbiased and will be the average of all the $X_i$ samples. In addition, if $X_i$ sample from a Bernoulli distribution, then $\mu_X=p$ and $\sigma^2_X=p(1-p)$.  Summing $n$ independent Bernoulli random variables gives us a Binomial distribution with variance $np(1-p)$.

By the central limit theorem, $$\frac{\hat p-\mu_X}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2_X}{n}}}=\frac{(Y/n)-p}{\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}}$$is approximately a standard normal distribution when $n$ is large enough. This means that we can find $z_{\alpha/2}$ such that $$P\left[-z_{\alpha/2}\leq\frac{(Y/n)-p}{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}\leq z_{\alpha/2}\right]\approx1-\alpha$$By swapping terms around and performing another approximation, we have bounds $(y/n)\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{(y/n)(1-(y/n))/n}$, where $Y$ is observed to be $y$.
