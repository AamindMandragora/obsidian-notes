In the previous distributions, we only had a single random variable $X$, but we'll often want to make predictions based on at least an additional variable $Y$. If $X$ and $Y$ are two discrete random variables, let $S$ denote the corresponding two-dimensional space of $X$ and $Y$ (made of tuples). The probability that $X=x$ and $Y=y$ is denoted by $P(X=x, Y=y)=f(x,y)$, the **joint probability mass function** of $X$ and $Y$, which satisfies the following properties: $0\leq f(x,y)\leq 1$; $\sum_{(x,y)\in S}f(x,y)=1$; and $P((X, Y)\in A)=\sum_{(x,y)\in A}f(x,y)$, where $A$ is a subset of the space $S$. A distribution of this kind is called a **discrete bivariate distribution**.

We can also derive the pmfs of $X$ and $Y$ from the joint pmf by holding $x$ and $y$ constant, then summing over all possible values of the other variable. More specifically, define $S_X$ and $S_Y$ as the supports of $X$ and $Y$, then $$P(X=x)=f_X(x)=\sum_{y\in S_Y}f(x, y)$$and $$P(Y=y)=f_Y(y)=\sum_{x\in S_X}f(x, y)$$$f_X$ and $f_Y$ are the **marginal probability mass functions** of $X$ and $Y$, respectively. $X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if for every $(x,y)\in S$, $P(X=x, Y=y)=P(X=x)P(Y=y)$, or equivalently, $f(x, y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y)$.
