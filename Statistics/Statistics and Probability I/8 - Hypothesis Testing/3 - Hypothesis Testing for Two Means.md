$H_0$ is now defined as $\mu_1=\mu_2$, or in other words, $\mu_d=\mu_1-\mu_2=0$, and $H_1:\mu_d > / < / \neq 0$.

Say we have two populations with means $\mu_1,\mu_2$ and variances $\sigma^2_1,\sigma^2_2$. Let $X_k$ and $Y_k$ be $n_1$ and $n)2$ independent samples from each of these populations, respectively. If $n_1$ and $n_2$ are large, then we can approximate a confidence interval for $\mu_1-\mu_2$: $$(\overline X+\overline Y)\pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2_1}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma^2_2}{n_2}}$$If the variances are unknown, we can just replace $z$ with $t$ and $\sigma$ with $s$. The conservative estimate for the degrees of freedom is the minimum degrees of freedom minus one, but if we assume both samples have the same variance, we can just say $df=n_1+n_2-2$ and create a pooled $$s^2=\frac{(n_1-1)s_1^2+(n_2-1)s^2_2}{n_1+n_2-2}$$If the ratio between the standard deviations is between $0.5$ and $2$, we can use that pooled $s$ as an estimator for both.

If the data is paired where the difference has mean $\delta_d$ and standard deviation $\sigma_d$, a confidence interval for $\sigma_d$ is $\overline D\pm t_{\alpha/2}\frac{s_d}{\sqrt n}$ and a good test statistic for $H_0:\delta =\delta_0$ is $T=\frac{\overline D-\sigma_0}{s_d/\sqrt n}$.
