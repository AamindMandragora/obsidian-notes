Recall that a binomial distribution takes fixed parameters $n$ and $p$ and gives the probability of $k$ successes in $n$ trials, where each trial has a $p$ chance to succeed. This assumes that, out of all of the successful outcomes and all of the unsuccessful outcomes, after one is selected it is then replaced.

Say we have $N_1$ success outcomes and $N_2$ fail outcomes, then let $X$ be the number of successful outcomes without replacement in a random sample of size $n$. Initially, $p=\frac{N_1}{N}$, where $N=N_1+N_2$, but as more outcomes are removed from the space, the probability changes. This distribution is called **hypergeometric**, and has the following properties: $$\begin{align}f(x)&=\frac{\binom{N_1}{x}\binom{N_2}{n-x}}{\binom{N}{n}}\text{, $x\leq n$, $x\leq N_1$, $n-x\leq N_2$} \\ E[X] &= n\frac{N_1}{N} \\ Var[X] &= n\frac{N_1}{N}\frac{N_2}{N}\frac{N-n}{N-1}\end{align}$$Also recall that a binomial distribution can only select between two classes of outcomes, usually success and failure. If we want more than that, we use a **multinomial distribution**, with parameters $n,p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_k$ for $k$ different classes. Therefore: $$\begin{align}f(x)&=f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_k)=n!\prod_{i=0}^k\frac{p_i^{x_i}}{x_i!} \\ E[X_i]&=np_i \\ Var[X_i]&=np_i(1-p_i)\end{align}$$Notice that these equation are very similar to the binomial distribution equations.
