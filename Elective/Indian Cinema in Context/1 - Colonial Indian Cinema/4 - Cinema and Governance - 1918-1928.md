British rulers were worried about India's growth in the film industry even before the advent of sound, which posed as large a problem as Hollywood did, and spent much of the 1920s desperately trying to influence and control their efforts. The first official measures, the Indian Cinematograph Act of 1918 and the 1920 regional censor boards, tried to force all aspects of Indian cinema under imperial scrutiny, but the measures fell short. Britain's economic ambitions were sated by the fast growth of the Indian market, and hoped to extract further resources.

Britain was concerned both about its place in the post-Great War world and the potential that the Indian masses could regain control of their country at any time. These fears were founded as, during the 1857 rebellion, called the "Mutiny" and the "first war of independence", Indian foot soldiers rebelled against the East India Company's army, which forced the transfer of Indian governance from said company to the Crown. This left an anxiety at all levels of British-Indian expression, culminating in these efforts to control all cultural production and expression, like the Indian Press act of 1867, which enforced control over print media.

The British colonial government saw all indigenous enterprises as a threat to their political control, especially the growing bourgeois Indian nationalism after the Great War and the Russian Revolution. The Indian Cinematograph Act of 1918, later amended in 1919 and 1920, reflected the Britishers' worry of the future while pretending to be concerned with safety and operational measures. It ended up being primarily a way to subject every film in India to governmental approval. That Indian bourgeoisie performed several more mutinies after the 1857 rebellion, leading to the foundation of the Indian National Congress as an interface between them and the government. The swadeshi movement mobilized the masses like no other in Bengal, and it was filmed by Jyotish Sarkar under J. F. Madan. The indigenous public sphere was created after watching Sen and Phalke's films, and further access to cinema grew it rapidly. Even the small amount of literate consumers of print media worried the British, while the mass of other potential consumers couldn't be recognized without accepting India's emergent nationalist identity or categorized in any way. Since the bottom couldn't be controlled, the British chose to apply restraints from the top down.

The next logical step was the 1920 censor boards set up in Bombay, Calcutta, Madras, and Rangoon
